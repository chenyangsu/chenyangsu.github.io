AI is making cyberattacks cheaper, faster, and more accessible

A software weakness can stay exactly the same and become more dangerous. Lower the cost of attacking it, and more people can afford to try.

Chen-Yang Su·3 November 2026· 

Main takeaways
  • Cheaper attempts change which targets are worth attacking. AI can reduce the labor needed to understand software and adapt tools.
  • Speed, access, and capability are different changes. Real incidents and controlled benchmarks provide evidence for different parts of the story.
  • Defenders can change the same calculation. Faster fixes and narrower permissions reduce the opportunities an attacker can use.

Your student club has a website. It holds event registrations and runs on a few donated cloud credits. You built it over a weekend with an AI coding assistant.

Imagine that it contains a bug that could expose the cloud account. Finding and using the bug would take an attacker a full afternoon. Your little website might not be worth the effort.

Now imagine that software can do most of that work in minutes. The website has not changed. Neither has the bug. The attack has become cheaper. Your account could become worth targeting for its computing credits, even if nobody cares about the club.

This is a hypothetical example, but it gives us a useful question to ask about AI and cybersecurity: what happens when an attack that used to require scarce expertise becomes affordable at scale?

A cheaper attempt can make an old weakness worth finding

Start with a simple economic model. An attacker compares the expected benefit of an attempt with its cost. If an attempt has a 10% chance of returning $100, its expected gross return is $10. An attempt costing $20 loses money on average. An attempt costing $2 has a positive expected return, before other costs.

These numbers are invented. The relationship is the point: reducing the cost can change whether the same target is worth attacking. The attacker does not need a better vulnerability for that to happen.

There are two ways activity could increase. An existing operator can afford more attempts with the same budget. Other people may start attempting attacks when the required skills and startup effort fall. The second effect depends on people choosing to enter; it does not follow automatically from a faster tool.

Cheaper tools therefore do not guarantee more successful breaches. They could produce many more failed attempts. Defenders could also reduce the probability of success enough to offset the increase. To understand what is actually happening, we need observations as well as a model.

The observed change is in the work between steps

A cyberattack involves several kinds of work. Reconnaissance means learning about a target. A vulnerability is a weakness that could permit an unauthorized action. An exploit is a way to use that weakness. Credentials, such as passwords and access tokens, can provide another route in.

Automation in attacks predates language models. What an AI agent can add is help with unfamiliar code and the transitions between tasks: interpreting an error, modifying a script, choosing another tool, and trying again. An agent is a model connected to tools and a loop that lets it act on results.

In a campaign observed in Q2 2026, Google's Threat Intelligence Group reported that attackers compromised a cloud resource and then planned, built, and ran an agent-enabled credential-harvesting campaign in under six hours. AI handled parts of the scanning workflow and troubleshooting. The report appeared on 8 September.

That is evidence of a fast, AI-assisted operation. It does not show how long the same operators would have taken without AI, or that AI caused the initial cloud compromise.

In a post dated 2 November UTC, Palo Alto Networks CEO Nikesh Arora wrote: “attacks are not becoming more sophisticated, it's the same attacks, faster and by a lot more people.” That describes one mechanism well. It is too broad as a description of every change in AI's offensive abilities.

Anthropic's September misuse report also describes individuals and groups using AI to coordinate work across targets. These are selected cases visible to a model provider. They support the existence of the mechanism; they are not a representative census of attackers.

New capabilities and wider access are separate developments

Some models are also getting better at difficult technical tasks. In April, Anthropic reported strong exploit-development results for Claude Mythos Preview and initially limited access through Project Glasswing. That concerns what a model can do.

By September, a second issue was clear: who could obtain similar capabilities. Open-weight means a model's learned parameters are available for others to download and run. It does not imply that all training data or development code is public, or that running the model is free.

NIST's Center for AI Standards and Innovation assessed Z.ai's GLM-5.3 as the most cyber-capable open-weight model released at the time. It still placed the model about four months behind the US frontier on its aggregate cybersecurity measure. That is a comparison across evaluations, not a forecast of when the gap will close.

Anthropic's separate evaluation found similar performance for GLM-5.3 and Mythos Preview on one exploit-development benchmark. Similarity on one test does not establish equal overall capability.

Three different questions

Speed: how much work can an operator complete in an hour? Capability: which tasks can the model solve? Access: who can obtain and run it? Progress on one can matter even when the other two stay fixed.

The defender is running a clock, too

Many attacks still have ordinary entry points. Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report says software vulnerability exploitation accounted for 31% of breaches in its reporting dataset, ahead of credential abuse. Its incident period was November 2024 through October 2025. Those dates make it useful background, not a measure of the effect of the 2026 models above.

A patch is a software update that fixes a problem. Once a flaw becomes known, attackers may investigate it while the organizations using the software test and install the fix. Faster attacker work can shorten the time available to act.

A trusted tool can carry the attack across a boundary

Your club app probably depends on libraries, a hosting provider, and a development extension. Those tools can carry permissions: access to files, repository contents, or deployment credentials. An attacker may reach you through one of them.

CISA's May 2026 bulletin about Nx Console describes a poisoned development extension delivered through automatic updates. CISA reported that the compromise reached a GitHub employee's device and led to theft from internal repositories. The bulletin establishes a supply-chain route; it does not establish that AI caused the incident.

A supply-chain attack compromises something the target depends on. Its impact depends on what that dependency can reach. In the illustration, change the permissions held by an already compromised tool.

AI agents add a related problem. They read repository files, tool responses, and external documents. An attacker can put instructions in that content and try to make the agent treat them as commands from its user. This is prompt injection: untrusted information attempts to become an instruction.

Google's September report documents malicious files aimed at coding assistants, as well as attempts to manipulate AI security scanners. A model's ability to read a file should not itself grant the file's author authority over the model's tools.

For a student building an app, “it works” is only the first test

Return to the club website. Suppose it correctly saves a registration and displays a confirmation. That checks the happy path. It does not tell you whether one member can read another member's private information.

Authentication asks who you are. Authorization asks what you are allowed to do. A functioning login screen can coexist with broken authorization. An AI-generated app needs both checked.

Several defensive choices follow from the mechanisms we have examined:

Reduce opportunities: know what is exposed and keep it maintained

For your own app, list the public services and software dependencies. Apply relevant fixes. CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog identifies flaws with evidence of exploitation; it helps prioritize work rather than treating every warning equally.

Reduce success: test permissions, including the denied actions

Use separate test accounts. Check that one member cannot read or change another's private record. Keep secrets out of browser-delivered code. Review changes affecting access rules. These checks address specific failure modes that a working demo can miss.

Reduce impact: give tools only the access their task needs

Keep production credentials away from experiments and unfamiliar repositories. Use a disposable environment for code you have not reviewed. A tool that can edit a test project need not also control the live database. The permission map shows why that boundary matters.

Use AI to help find and fix weaknesses, then verify the fix

A defender can ask an assistant to explain a dependency warning, inspect an access rule, or propose a test. Verify the result against the actual application. More generated findings help only when someone can check, prioritize, and resolve them.

What would establish the size of the effect?

The reports give us concrete examples of AI assisting real attacks. The evaluations show that some models can solve difficult exploitation tasks. The economic model explains why lower costs could expand activity. Those are different kinds of evidence.

To estimate how much AI increases successful breaches, we would need stronger comparisons: comparable operators working with and without AI, measured labor and computing costs, and outcomes across representative targets. We would also need to account for changes in defenses and reporting. Selected incident reports do not provide that full comparison.

The same economic logic applies to defenders. If AI makes finding a flaw cheap but reviewing and deploying a fix remains slow, the defender's bottleneck persists. If it helps verify and ship fixes sooner, the window in Figure 4 shrinks.

For the student club, the useful change is concrete: review who can access registration data, keep deployment credentials out of the coding agent's workspace, and verify the relevant fixes. These actions change the attacker's chance of success and the damage a compromised tool can cause. In the budget example, a tenfold fall in attack cost was exactly offset by a tenfold fall in success probability.
