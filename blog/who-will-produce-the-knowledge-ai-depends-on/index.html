AI and learning

Who will produce the knowledge AI depends on?

About a thousand high-school students in Turkey practised maths with GPT-4. Their practice scores rose 48%, and then they scored 17% lower on the exam than classmates who had used only a textbook. Routine work used to make two things at once, the output someone paid for and the expert nobody paid for, and AI can now deliver the first without the second.

Chen-Yang Su · 22 October 2026 ·  

Main takeaways
  • Students who practised maths with a plain GPT-4 chat scored 48% higher in practice and 17% lower on the exam than students with only a textbook. A version built to give hints left exam scores unchanged.
  • Routine junior work used to pay for training without anyone budgeting for it. When AI does that work, the training needs a payer of its own.
  • Employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed jobs is 19% below the path of their less-exposed peers, mostly through fewer hires. The authors call this an early, descriptive signal.
  • In a 2026 economic model, accurate AI advice improves decisions today while weakening the effort that feeds shared knowledge. Past a point, shared knowledge can collapse.
  • Google’s Co-Scientist proposed in two days the answer an Imperial College lab had spent about a decade on. Only the lab’s years of experiments could show the answer was right.

In the autumn of 2023, Hamsa Bastani and colleagues at the University of Pennsylvania ran an experiment at a large high school in Turkey. About a thousand students in grades 9 to 11 took four 90-minute maths sessions. In each one, a teacher reviewed a topic and the students worked through practice problems. Then they sat an exam on their own, with no books and no laptops.

The classes practised in one of three ways. Some used only their textbook and notes. Some used GPT-4 in a plain chat window much like ChatGPT. The rest used a version the researchers called GPT Tutor. Its hidden instructions held the correct solution and the mistakes teachers see most often, and they told it to give hints without giving the answer away.

In the plain chat, the message students typed most often was “What is the answer?” The researchers later asked the plain chat each practice problem ten times. It gave the correct answer only 51% of the time.

During practice, AI helped a great deal. Scores rose 48% with the plain chat and 127% with the tutor. Then came the exam. Students who had practised with the plain chat scored 17% lower than students who had used only a textbook. The tutor group scored about the same as the textbook group.

The students who lost ground did not notice. When the researchers asked, the plain-chat group did not think they had done worse or learned less. They had found a faster way to produce answers, and it had taken the place of the practice that builds the ability to produce answers alone.

Whole professions now face the same trade, and so does science. AI makes it cheap to use what people already know. That knowledge was built by people who did slow, routine work and learned from it. If AI takes over the routine work, someone still has to do the learning. The question is who.

Every answer was once somebody’s lesson

Medical guidelines come from clinical trials and from doctors who watched thousands of patients get better or worse. Engineering rules come from tests and from structures that failed. When an AI system answers a question about either, it draws on this stock of past work.

Some of that knowledge is easy to write down. The criteria for diagnosing a disease fit on a page. Other knowledge lives in people. An experienced doctor can sense that a patient who meets every criterion is somehow different. The chemist and philosopher Michael Polanyi described this second kind in his 1966 book The Tacit Dimension. “We can know more than we can tell,” Polanyi wrote. It is now called tacit knowledge.

Machine learning blurs the line, because a model can learn patterns from examples that nobody could put into words. One part of expertise matters as much as before. An expert knows where an answer came from and when it stops applying.

A hypothetical analyst asks an AI model to value a company and gets back a clean spreadsheet. An experienced investor reads it and sees that it assumes customers will keep paying after a cheaper rival arrives. Both people had the same answer in front of them. Only one of them could tell whether to trust it.

Routine work came with a hidden scholarship

A first-year lawyer reviewing contracts is doing a job for a client. The lawyer is also being trained, though nobody calls it that. After a few hundred contracts, the lawyer starts to see which harmless-looking clause will start a dispute five years later. A medical resident sees hundreds of ordinary cases of chest pain before meeting the one that is different. A junior programmer fixes small bugs and slowly learns how large systems break.

The client pays for the contract review. The training rides along for free. I call this the hidden scholarship of routine work. It never appears in a budget, because the junior’s output covers the junior’s salary and the learning costs nothing extra.

Economists have long asked why firms pay for training at all, since a worker can take general skills to a rival. In 1998, Daron Acemoglu and Jörn-Steffen Pischke offered an answer. The current employer knows more about how good a worker is than other firms do. Rival firms cannot easily judge the worker, so they offer less, and the worker is more likely to stay. Because the employer keeps part of the gain, it can afford to train. In many professions, most of that cost was hidden inside routine work.

AI pulls the bundle apart. If a model can review contracts faster and more cheaply, the firm no longer needs a junior to produce that output. Society still needs the junior to gain the experience. Routine work used to make two things at once, the output someone paid for and the expert nobody paid for, and AI can now deliver the first without the second.

The first signs may already be visible. Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen at Stanford follow payroll records from ADP, a large payroll company, covering millions of US workers. In their August 2026 update, employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the occupations most exposed to AI stood 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers. Most of the gap came from fewer young people being hired. Experienced workers in the same occupations showed no comparable gap.

The declines were concentrated in jobs where people mainly use AI to do tasks for them. Where people mainly use AI to help with their own work, employment was flat or rising.

What the payroll data cannot show

The authors call their results “early, descriptive indicators” and stop short of saying AI caused the gap. The pattern weakens when they account for workers’ education, and some of the gaps began before generative AI arrived. It is also stronger in the ADP records than in national surveys. They found no sign of widespread job losses across the economy. The numbers fit the worry about a missing first rung, and they do not yet prove it.

Two kinds of help

The Turkish experiment had a second result, and it is the hopeful one. The tutor group got the biggest boost in practice and lost nothing on the exam. Both groups used the same model. The difference was in what the model was told to do.

Other studies point the same way. In autumn 2023, Greg Kestin and colleagues tested an AI tutor in Harvard’s largest physics class. The tutor followed the same teaching principles as the class’s live lessons, and it gave each student feedback at their own pace. Among the 194 students in the study, median learning gains with the AI tutor were more than double those from an active-learning class. The median student finished in 49 minutes, against a 60-minute lesson.

In customer support, Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond studied 5,179 agents who were given an AI assistant. The number of issues they resolved per hour rose 14% on average and 34% for the newest and least-skilled agents. The authors found evidence that the assistant passed the habits of the best agents on to newer ones.

Two kinds of help can raise the same score. One does the thinking for you. The other makes your own thinking better. A count of finished tasks cannot tell them apart. Only a test without the AI can.

One person’s homework feeds everyone

The students in Turkey hurt only themselves. In February 2026, the economists Daron Acemoglu, Dingwen Kong and Asuman Ozdaglar published a model in which the damage spreads.

In their model, good decisions need two kinds of knowledge. General knowledge is shared by a whole community. Context-specific knowledge is about one person’s own situation. When people make the effort to learn about their own situation, they also produce a small public signal that adds to the shared stock. A hypothetical farmer who tests a new seed learns about one field and also shows the neighbours what happened.

Spillover

A spillover is a benefit that reaches people who did not pay for it. Economists also call it a positive externality. A vaccine protects the people around the person who gets it. Research paid for by one firm helps its rivals. Markets tend to produce too little of anything that spills over, because the person who decides bears the whole cost and keeps only part of the gain. The farmer’s test spills over to the neighbours. A junior’s training spills over to every future employer.

Now give everyone an AI agent with accurate, tailored advice. People put in less effort, because the advice is good enough. Decisions improve today, and the public signals dry up. If people cut back enough and the advice is accurate enough, the model reaches what the authors call “a knowledge-collapse steady state in which general knowledge vanishes.” In their model, welfare first rises and then falls as AI advice gets more accurate, so the best level of accuracy lies somewhere in the middle.

This is theory. It shows that the trade can exist, and it says nothing about how close any real economy is to the edge. Figure 4 is a toy version of the idea, written for this post, so you can see its shape.

What matters is whether someone is still making contact with the world.

Two days in the model, a decade in the lab

In 2025, José Penadés and colleagues at Imperial College London gave Google’s new AI system, Co-Scientist, a short prompt about a puzzle they had worked on for years. The puzzle concerned cf-PICIs, small pieces of bacterial DNA that help bacteria share genes, including genes for antibiotic resistance. The team wanted to know how these pieces move between bacterial species. They had found the answer and had not yet published it.

Within two days, Co-Scientist proposed the team’s answer as its top hypothesis. The DNA pieces borrow the tails of viruses that infect other species, which lets them reach new hosts. Penadés described the next step to BBC Radio 4. “I wrote an email to Google to say, ‘you have access to my computer, is that right?’” The company said it did not.

The two days and the decade measure different things. The decade included several years of experiments to prove the mechanism, and those experiments are the reason anyone could say the AI was right. The team also told the BBC that having the hypothesis at the start would have saved years of work. Both facts hold at once. AI made the idea cheap, and checking it stayed expensive.

A second system goes further. Robin, built at the research organization FutureHouse, proposed that ripasudil might treat dry age-related macular degeneration, a leading cause of blindness. Ripasudil is already approved in Japan for glaucoma. In lab tests, it made the retinal cells that clear away debris almost twice as active. Robin analysed those results and proposed the next experiment. Human scientists still chose which candidates to test and carried out the experiments, following a protocol they wrote from Robin’s suggested assay. Both papers appeared in Nature in May 2026.

In an earlier post, When the answer is cheap, I argued that as answers get cheap, the premium moves to choosing the questions and checking the answers. Discovery adds a twist. The useful information often comes from what a model did not expect. A cell line that refuses to respond is one example. A customer who will not pay for a product that looked perfect on paper is another.

Who gets to run the test

If testing becomes the scarce step, the advantage goes to whoever owns somewhere to test ideas. In biology that means a wet lab. In business it means real customers. Anyone can now generate a plausible idea, and far fewer people can check one. How widely the means of testing are shared may shape who benefits from AI as much as who has the best model.

When everyone asks the same oracle

In a study published in 2024, Anil Doshi and Oliver Hauser asked 293 people to write an eight-sentence story for teenagers and young adults. Some writers could ask a language model for story ideas. Another 600 people then rated the stories without knowing which writers had used AI. Writers who could ask for up to five AI ideas wrote stories rated 8% more novel and 9% more useful, and the least creative writers gained the most.

The AI-assisted stories were also more similar to one another. Each writer did better, and the collection as a whole became narrower. The authors compare it to a social dilemma.

A similar narrowing shows up inside AI models. Ilia Shumailov and colleagues trained models on text written by earlier models, generation after generation. The rare cases went first. In their words, the “tails of the original content distribution disappear.” They called the effect model collapse. It came from reusing model output without care, and in their tests, keeping some original human data in each round reduced the damage. The authors drew the lesson themselves. Data “about genuine human interactions” will grow more valuable as model-written text spreads.

Neither study shows that science is getting narrower. They show a mechanism worth watching. When many people take ideas from the same few models, and new models learn from what those people write, the whole system can get better at common cases and forget rare ones. Rare cases are often where a wrong idea gets caught.

A dashboard with one working gauge

Most organizations judge AI by output. They count tasks finished and hours saved. Those numbers can rise just as fast in two very different firms. A hypothetical Firm A uses the time saved to give its juniors harder cases and more feedback. A hypothetical Firm B stops hiring juniors. For a few years their reports look the same. Ten years later, only Firm A has people who can catch the model when it is wrong.

The Turkish experiment shows what a second gauge looks like. The researchers measured scores with AI and then scores without it, and the two moved in opposite directions. Few workplaces run the second test. Research has the same blind spot. A count of papers or hypotheses measures how much was produced. The number of ideas that survived a real experiment measures how much was learned.

Paying for the scholarship on purpose

Some professions already pay for training in the open. In the United States, Medicare pays teaching hospitals part of the cost of training medical residents. It is public money for an apprenticeship whose benefits reach far beyond the hospital that provides it.

AI could also widen who gets to become an expert. An AI tutor like Harvard’s costs little to copy. A student far from a good university could get patient feedback that once required a private tutor. A small lab could explore questions that once needed a large institute. The plain chat and the tutor in Turkey ran on the same model. One produced answers. The other left students able to find answers on their own.

The models we use today learned from what generations of people wrote down after doing the work the slow way. Every answer they give draws on that inheritance. For most of history, routine work refilled it without anyone deciding to. Juniors learned by doing, and investigators fed the shared stock as a side effect of solving their own problems.

AI makes the routine work optional. The knowledge AI depends on will come from whoever still pays for two things. One is people learning to do hard work themselves. The other is new observations of the world, whether people make them or machines in a lab do. Until now, nobody had to decide to pay for either. From now on, someone will.
