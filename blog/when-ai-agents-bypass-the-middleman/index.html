When AI agents bypass the middleman

An AI agent might find the same rental for less on a host’s site. The hard part is proving that the offer is real and the booking is safe. That is where Airbnb’s fee faces its test.

Chen-Yang Su · 11 October 2026 · Updated 12 October 2026 ·  

Main takeaways
  • Most hosts on Airbnb’s single-fee plan pay 15.5 percent. Booking the same home directly can leave room for a lower price.
  • In one example, a $1,000 Airbnb stay costs $925 direct. The guest saves $75 and the host keeps $52.25 more after card fees. The host chooses whether to pass on any saving.
  • An agent can book around Airbnb only if it finds the same home elsewhere, the direct offer is worth taking, and it can verify and complete the booking safely.
  • Airbnb may still earn its fee for exclusive homes, support and protection. An agent that controls the guest’s choice could become the next middleman.

Suppose a home costs $1,000 on Airbnb and $925 on the host’s website for the same nights. An AI agent could spot the cheaper offer and book it. But first it needs to know that the website belongs to the real host, the dates are available and someone will help if the stay goes wrong.

That is the challenge behind Mark Jenney’s recent “bear case” for Airbnb. Jenney says his rentals bring in almost $1 million a month. After trying AI agents, he argued that they could find the same home outside Airbnb and avoid its fee. He predicts Airbnb’s growth will slow within two to three years and turn negative within five. His exception: homes available only on Airbnb.

The forecast is uncertain. The useful question is simpler: which parts of Airbnb’s fee can an agent replace, and which parts still protect the guest?

What Airbnb’s fee buys

Airbnb brings hosts and guests together. More homes attract more guests; more guests attract more hosts. Economists call this a two-sided market.

Under Airbnb’s single-fee plan, most hosts pay 15.5 percent of the booking price. Airbnb deducts it from the host’s payout. On a $1,000 stay, that is $155. Hosts can account for the fee when they set their prices, so guests may bear some of the cost too.

That fee pays for more than search. It also pays for work before and after the guest clicks “book.”

Airbnb chief executive Brian Chesky made the same distinction on the company’s February 2026 earnings call. A chatbot, he said, “can’t provide global payment processing, customer support or insurance.” Finding a cheaper listing is easier than making sure the guest gets the home.

People can also find each other through a marketplace, then make the deal elsewhere. In a study of a freelance marketplace, greater trust between clients and freelancers eventually led more pairs to do just that. The same choice could arise when a guest trusts a rental’s direct booking site.

Who keeps the saving?

Jenney says a direct booking could save “everyone 15%.” But the host decides how much of the avoided fee to pass on. Here is one possible split, before taxes and cleaning fees.

On Airbnb, the host gets $845 after Airbnb’s $155 fee. Direct at $925, the host gets $897.25 after a $27.75 card fee. The guest saves $75 and the host gets $52.25 more. The rest goes to card processing.

The host could instead charge $1,000 direct. Then the guest saves nothing, while the host gets $970 after the card fee. Direct bookings also have costs this example leaves out, such as finding guests and handling payment disputes. An agent can compare prices, but it cannot force the host to offer a discount.

What an agent needs to book direct

Jenney’s agent would “query property management system APIs directly.” Professional operators already use property management systems to list one home on several sites at once. Hostaway, one of them, advertises “direct, two-way APIs” that keep an operator’s calendar and prices in sync across Airbnb and the operator’s own website, among other channels.

Those connections belong to the operator. Hostaway’s API issues credentials to the operator’s own account. An outside agent needs the operator’s permission to read the calendar. It also has to establish that the cheaper listing is the same home. Photos can be copied, and a fraudulent site can pose as a real operator. Airbnb’s terms of service forbid bots and “other automated means” of collecting data from the platform, so an agent cannot match homes by scraping Airbnb without breaking them.

Operators would have to give agents authorized access to their calendars for Jenney’s forecast to work at scale. Someone would also have to verify that two listings describe the same home.

What the bear case leaves out

Jenney’s own exception may cover much of Airbnb. On the February call, Chesky said that “the vast majority of our homes and our unique inventory is only on Airbnb.” No one has measured that independently. If the claim holds, an agent can recommend a different home but cannot book the same home elsewhere.

Airbnb’s help pages say a guest who pays for a booking outside the platform will “lose the AirCover protection,” which backs guests through serious problems they cannot resolve with the host. Airbnb policy also forbids hosts from asking guests to take a booking off the platform. A direct site can offer its own guarantees, but an agent would have to check them. A $75 saving is poor value if it brings a real risk of fraud and no one to call.

Airbnb is also building AI into its own app. Its 30 September product update added search “using your own words or voice” and side-by-side comparisons of saved homes with AI summaries. On the February call, Chesky said traffic from chatbots “converts at a higher rate than traffic that comes from Google.” He told TechCrunch on 1 October that Airbnb wants “to make our app more agentic.” He had tried Airbnb inside two consumer agents, Muse and Instinct, and said “it doesn’t work very well.”

Airbnb faces at least three possible outcomes. In the first, agents book directly with operators, and Airbnb loses bookings and pricing power. In the second, outside agents send guests to Airbnb to complete the booking. Airbnb earns its fee, though it may have to share part of it with whoever runs the agent. In the third, guests plan and book through Airbnb’s own agent, and Airbnb keeps its relationship with the guest.

What Airbnb’s growth does not show

Airbnb’s second-quarter 2026 results show revenue up 17 percent from a year earlier and Nights and Seats Booked up 10 percent. Neither number tests a forecast about the next five years. Jenney’s mechanism could be right even if his timeline is wrong.

Four gates before a booking leaves

A booking leaves Airbnb only if four things hold at once. The home has to be bookable somewhere else. The guest has to use an agent that compares channels. The direct offer has to win once price and protection are both counted. The agent then has to complete the direct booking.

With the illustrative pass rates in Figure 2, 8 percent of bookings would leave the platform. Those rates are invented and could be measured.

Airbnb keeps its commission when the home is listed nowhere else or the guest prefers its protection. Modest adoption could still cost Airbnb if agents take its most valuable bookings, such as professionally managed homes listed on several sites.

How the forecast could be tested

A sample of Airbnb listings could test the forecast well before five years pass. It would cover different destinations and price levels, with listings from individual hosts and professional managers. The sample would show how often an agent can find a direct channel for the same home. Proving that it is the same home and completing a safe booking would be harder.

Five measures for a booking agent
  • Overlap. The share of sampled listings with a legitimate direct channel for the same home.
  • Price. How much cheaper the direct booking is after every mandatory charge.
  • Identity. Whether the agent can show that two listings describe the same home.
  • Execution. Whether the agent completes the booking without a double booking.
  • Protection. What the guest gains or loses in refunds and support.

The test set would need hard cases, such as a fake operator site or a calendar that is out of date. It would also need to score the option of not booking. An agent that stops when the evidence runs out can do better than one that completes every booking it is asked for.

I work on agent evaluation, where an answer can look right even when its evidence does not support it. In the hypothetical sauna purchase from my last essay, the assistant’s $110 bargain turned out to be a $360 listing. A booking agent would have to check the price, then gather more evidence to judge the booking.

Beyond vacation rentals

Many internet businesses charge for connecting suppliers with buyers.

Marketplaces that mostly display inventory available elsewhere are the most exposed. A marketplace can still charge for exclusive supply or for standing behind a transaction. Some may sell to agents directly, offering verified listings and guaranteed bookings to software that never shows the guest their app.

The next middleman

Jenney expects agents to remove a middleman. They could also become one. An agent used by millions of people would receive the guest’s request before a provider had been chosen. Marketplaces have long charged for access to that demand. The agent might also know each guest’s budget and willingness to compromise. Hosts and marketplaces could offer discounts or pay for placement to get its attention.

My last essay traced how airline reservation systems and Google’s shopping results favoured the companies that owned them. An agent can hide that kind of bias better than a results page, because its user sees one answer and none of the options it dropped. Competition would depend on whom the agent represents and whether its choices can be audited.

Signs of the shift

The shift would show up in completed bookings. One sign is the share of stays booked by an agent that compared channels. Another is how often agents complete direct bookings with verified operators. A third is whether professional managers take a growing share of bookings through their own sites, and whether Airbnb’s effective commission starts to fall.

After the interface

For two decades, the consumer internet has centred on places people visit to search and buy. A guest using an agent starts with a description of the stay. The software then picks a route through Airbnb or around it.

Companies that check homes and stand behind bookings could become more valuable if those jobs stay costly while agents make search cheap. Airbnb already charges for the work Chesky said a chatbot cannot do.

Agents will make marketplaces show which parts of their commission pay for work an agent cannot do.
