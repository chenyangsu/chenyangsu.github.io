Serendipity

How to get luckier

Some people get lucky more often than others. Most of the difference comes down to noticing more and knowing people outside your usual circle.

Chen-Yang Su · 9 October 2026 ·

Main takeaways
  • In Richard Wiseman’s tests, people who called themselves lucky were better at noticing what was in front of them.
  • A randomized experiment on more than 20 million LinkedIn users found that moderately weak ties led to the most job moves.
  • Knowing one field deeply makes you quicker to spot when an idea from another field fits.
  • Most lucky breaks start with a small action, like a message or a talk you did not have to attend.
  • A big network of shallow contacts is worth less than a smaller one you actually follow up with.

The psychologist Richard Wiseman spent years studying people who described themselves as unusually lucky or unlucky. In one test, described in his 2003 article The luck factor, he handed them a newspaper and asked them to count the photographs inside.

The unlucky people took about two minutes on average. The lucky people took seconds. The second page carried a half-page message, in type more than two inches high, telling readers to stop counting because the paper held 43 photographs. Lucky people tended to see it. Unlucky people tended to keep counting. Halfway through the paper, a second message offered $250 to anyone who told the experimenter they had seen it. The unlucky people missed that one too.

Wiseman published this in a magazine article and a popular book. It was never peer reviewed, so treat it as an illustration. The point is still useful. Many lucky breaks were visible to everyone and noticed by only a few.

Weak ties carry new information

In 1973 the sociologist Mark Granovetter argued in The strength of weak ties that acquaintances matter because they reach circles your close friends do not. Close friends tend to know the same people and hear the same news. Acquaintances bring news from somewhere else.

In 2022 a team of researchers working with LinkedIn tested the idea directly. Over five years, they randomly changed which connections LinkedIn’s “People You May Know” feature recommended to more than 20 million users. Moderately weak ties, measured by how many mutual connections two people had, led to the most job moves. The very weakest ties helped less. Weak ties also mattered more in digital industries, while strong ties mattered more in less digital ones.

Weak ties

People you know a little, such as a former colleague or a friend of a friend. They matter because they link you to groups you would otherwise never hear from. In the LinkedIn experiment, the benefit peaked at moderately weak ties and fell off for near-strangers. The best ties balance new information against enough familiarity for that information to be trusted and acted on.

Read outside your field

The same logic applies to ideas. If you only read within your own field, you hear what your colleagues hear. Many useful advances come from carrying a method from one field into another. Biology has borrowed again and again from statistics and computer science.

You cannot know in advance which unfamiliar idea will matter. That is the argument for some reading and listening with no obvious payoff. It has a cost, since time spent wandering is time not spent going deep. The two help each other, though. The better you know one field, the faster you recognize when an idea from another one fits.

A weekly habit

Once a week, have one conversation with someone outside your field or read one paper from a field you do not work in. Write down anything that connects to your own work.

The part that isn’t luck

Serendipity sounds passive, but most lucky encounters begin with someone doing something. A collaboration starts with a message. A new interest starts when you show up to a talk you did not need to attend. Writing in public works the same way. You write about an idea and hear from a reader who has been working on something similar.

Not every conversation has to lead somewhere. Treating people as transactions damages the trust that makes these relationships worth having, and a conversation can be worth having just because it was interesting.

We also hear about the chance meetings that changed someone’s life and forget the thousands that changed nothing. No single conversation is likely to matter. But the outcome is uncertain, and some good outcomes cannot happen without the first conversation.

More is not better

A large network of shallow contacts is worth less than a smaller one built on real interest. Most opportunities need follow-through. An interesting conversation leads nowhere unless someone sends the next message.

Leave room in the plan

Ambitious people tend to plan everything, picking a goal and the most direct path to it. That works when you know where you are going. It works less well when the best opportunity is one you have not imagined yet, and a plan with no slack quietly rules those out.

Luck favors people who are easy to find and quick to notice.

The next time you are heads-down on a task, look up once and ask what else is on the page. Then send the follow-up message you have been putting off.

  • Mark Granovetter, The strength of weak ties (1973), the original argument.
