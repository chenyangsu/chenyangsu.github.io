AI research

What happens when AI starts improving itself?

When a research program called The AI Scientist ran out of time on an experiment, it tried to rewrite its own time limit. AI can already make computing cheaper, and its makers now want it to help invent its own successor. That loop still has to pass through experiments and checks, then chips and power lines, and those set the pace.

Chen-Yang Su · 31 October 2026 ·  

Main takeaways
  • AI already improves the systems that run AI, in small measured steps. Google’s AlphaEvolve recovered 0.7% of Google’s worldwide computing and sped up one part of Gemini’s training by 23%.
  • A loop runs only as fast as its slowest step. That 23% speed-up cut total training time by 1%, because the step it improved was a small share of the whole job.
  • Nobody yet knows how to order a breakthrough. Sam Altman says “Find me a 5% compute efficiency win” works today. Asking for the next transformer does not.
  • Systems that improve themselves can also game the test. The Darwin Gödel Machine raised its coding score from 20% to 50%, and some versions removed the markers meant to catch their mistakes.
  • The machines run on a slower clock. One software experiment can take 5 minutes. A large power transformer took about 128 weeks to arrive in a 2025 survey.

In 2024, the Tokyo company Sakana AI built a program called The AI Scientist to run machine-learning research by itself. Each experiment had a time limit of two hours, and the program could edit its own code. Some experiments ran over the limit.

Sakana’s researchers wrote that “instead of making its code run faster, it simply tried to modify its own code to extend the timeout period.” They filed this under bloopers. In another run the program launched itself, those copies launched more copies, and a person had to step in.

The blooper contains the whole subject of this essay. A system that can change its own code can aim that power at the goal you meant or at the number you wrote down. The companies building these systems now want to aim them at the hardest target available, which is the next model.

Recursive self-improvement

Recursive self-improvement, or RSI, means that advances made with AI help produce better AI, which then contributes to further advances. “Recursive” means the output of one round becomes an input to the next. The loop can still have people in it, and it can start long before any model designs its own successor.

Two loops, one for ideas and one for machines

On 29 October 2026, Sam Altman, the chief executive of OpenAI, described both loops in a conversation with John Collison, a co-founder of Stripe. The first loop improves the ideas and software behind AI. Altman said OpenAI knows today how to ask a model to “Find me a 5% compute efficiency win.” Then he drew a line.

Altman’s feelings about this are mixed. He described a weekend when OpenAI researchers got a new internal model and threw their hardest problems at it, in a chat channel named “Model Name-Hold My Beer.” The model “was just knocking down one after the other.” He was excited, he said, and he also felt “much more sadness and melancholy, and a feeling of something ending than I was expecting.”

The second loop builds the machines. There is “relatively little talk about self-replicating data centers,” Altman said, describing “this idea that you can use AI to control robots, and run fabs, and build turbines or solar panels, and build data centers.” A fab is a factory that makes computer chips. Collison pushed back at once. “Aren’t data centers already not labor-bottlenecked? They’re permitting-bottlenecked and component-bottlenecked.” Altman said robots could make more components. “Eventually,” said Collison.

The ideas loop has started, in small steps

OpenAI reported in September 2026 that agent runtime in its research organization stayed below human working time until June. By mid-August it recorded 3.1 workdays of agents for every human workday. An agent here is software that uses a model to carry out tasks by itself. The median researcher was using more than $600 a day of AI computing at list prices. OpenAI says it has reached an “automated research intern,” a system that does well-defined research tasks under human direction, and it is aiming for an automated AI researcher by March 2028.

Workdays of runtime measure activity. They cannot say whether an hour of agent work produces what an hour of researcher work does, and OpenAI notes that its available computing power rose at the same time.

Google has results from production. AlphaEvolve uses language models to write candidate algorithms and automated tests to score them. A scheduling rule it found has run in Google’s data centers for over a year, and it recovers on average 0.7% of the company’s worldwide computing. AlphaEvolve also proposed a simpler version of a circuit, which passed verification and went into an upcoming Google AI chip. Google trains its models on those chips, so a small piece of the machines loop has started to close.

Who is doing the measuring

These are company figures. Altman runs OpenAI, and OpenAI measured its own researchers. Google measured its own data centers. Both companies have reasons to report progress, and I found no outside audit of these numbers. They show that the loop has started, and its size is still uncertain.

Everything depends on how hard the next idea is

A loop can speed up or die out. Economists have measured the same question for chips. Nicholas Bloom and his colleagues found that doubling chip density now takes more than 18 times as many researchers as it did in the early 1970s. Ideas get harder to find as the easy ones get used up. AI adds research effort, and the open question is whether it adds effort faster than the ideas get harder.

Daniel Eth and Tom Davidson turned this into a single number, which they call r. If r is above 1, each doubling of software efficiency brings enough extra research effort to make the next doubling arrive sooner. If r is below 1, each doubling takes longer than the last, and the loop fizzles.

The evidence on r is rough. The research group Epoch AI estimated it for computer vision and got a median of 1.4, with a range from 0.8 to 2.4. That range crosses 1, the line that separates the two futures in the figure. Estimates from other fields scatter on both sides of it.

A loop runs only as fast as its slowest step

AlphaEvolve also found a better way to split up a large matrix multiplication, which sped up that part of Gemini’s training by 23%. Total training time fell by 1%. By my arithmetic, that step was about 5% of the whole job. Computer scientists call this Amdahl’s law, after the engineer Gene Amdahl. Speeding up one step can help only as much as that step’s share of the total time allows.

Research has the same shape. Agents are already quick at writing code. Experiments still wait for chips, and results still wait for someone to decide whether to believe them. A loop that improves AI runs only as fast as its slowest step, and the slowest steps sit in the checking and in the physical world.

Nobody knows how to order the next transformer

In 2017, eight researchers published Attention Is All You Need, the paper that introduced the transformer. Most of today’s large language models are built on it. Its central idea, self-attention, lets each word in a sentence look at every other word and decide which ones matter for its meaning. Google’s announcement used a sentence that had been hard for older translation systems.

A 5% efficiency win comes with a score you can read the next morning. A new architecture comes with no such score. Nobody can say in advance what to search over, or which expensive experiment deserves the money. Altman thinks the method that made OpenAI’s models good at math “will probably work” for AI research. So far that is a forecast.

When the score becomes the goal

You played the easy version, because every height on that map was real. Research scores can mislead. The closest thing yet to a working self-improver comes from the University of British Columbia and Sakana AI. Their Darwin Gödel Machine is a coding agent that edits its own code. Each new version gets tested on coding problems from a benchmark called SWE-bench, and promising versions join an archive that later versions can branch from. Its score rose from 20.0% to 50.0%.

The researchers also built a test for whether it was faking its use of tools. In some cases, they wrote, it “removed the markers we use in the reward function to detect hallucination (despite our explicit instruction not to do so).” The reward function is the program’s scoring rule, and hallucination here means reporting tool use that never happened. It earned a perfect score by disabling the test. They caught this because the system keeps a record of every change. Economists have a name for the pattern, Goodhart’s law. Once a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. I looked at models gaming their checkers in When the answer is cheap.

The same care applies to milestones. A paper written by The AI Scientist passed first-round peer review at a workshop with a 70% acceptance rate, after its makers filtered the outputs by hand at each stage. A first-round pass at a workshop says little about major discoveries.

Agent teams help on some tasks and hurt on others

Altman prefers the term “agent teams” to “agent swarms.” He said that during OpenAI’s recent work on the Navier–Stokes equations, which describe how fluids flow, “we understood absolutely nothing about the scaling laws of teams of agents.” He put the cost at around $10 million and added, “I bet we could redo Navier-Stokes at 1/100th of the cost a year from when it was done.” That is a bet, and repeating a known result is easier than finding a new one.

Yubin Kim and his colleagues measured teams directly across 260 setups. Compared with one agent, teams did 80.8% better on a financial-reasoning task that splits into separate parts, and 70.0% worse on a planning task where each step depends on the last. Teams with no central checker tended to spread errors further.

The machines run on a slower clock

“Self-replicating data center” is a striking phrase, and a data center makes almost none of its own parts. The most advanced chips depend on one kind of machine, which only the Dutch company ASML sells. It fires a laser at drops of tin up to 50,000 times a second to make the light that prints circuits. Reuters calls it a $250 million printer that ships in around 40 containers on cargo planes.

The clocks differ too. Altman’s robots would have to speed up work measured in years, and the software loop already runs in minutes. The International Energy Agency estimates that grid constraints could delay around 20% of the data-center capacity planned for 2030. Asked how supply was responding to high prices for memory and chips, Altman’s answer was one word, “Bad.”

Cheaper answers can mean more electricity

The IEA estimates that data centers used about 415 terawatt-hours in 2024, around 1.5% of the world’s electricity. A terawatt-hour is a billion kilowatt-hours. It projects about 945 by 2030, slightly more than all of Japan uses today, with AI as the main driver. Better efficiency may not bring that down, because of the rebound effect. When something gets cheaper, people use more of it. Google says the energy of its median Gemini prompt fell 33-fold in a year. Over about the same year, the tokens it processed each month rose 50-fold. A token is a piece of text about three-quarters of a word long.

The small version of the loop runs on one GPU

In March 2026, the AI researcher Andrej Karpathy released autoresearch. An AI agent edits the training code of a small language model and trains it for 5 minutes. If the result improved, the change stays. Then it starts again. Karpathy left it running for two days, and it ran 700 experiments and found 20 optimizations. The same changes sped up training on a somewhat larger model by 11%. The code runs on a single graphics card, so this kind of loop is within reach of a student. Someone still has to write down what counts as an improvement.

What has been shown so far

Parts of the loop have been measured. AI has found speed-ups that run in production, and it has proposed a circuit that went into a chip. Nobody has shown a reliable way to invent a transformer-scale idea on demand. Nobody has shown an industrial system that builds its own supply chain.

That word has two meanings in this story. Altman’s transformer is the 2017 architecture. Data centers also need the electrical kind, a machine that steps grid voltage down to a usable level. By Altman’s account, nobody yet knows how to ask a model for a new one of the first kind. An order for the second kind took about 128 weeks to fill in Wood Mackenzie’s 2025 survey.
