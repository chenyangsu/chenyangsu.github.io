The uncomfortable truth about power: why merit alone is not enough

In 1847 Ignaz Semmelweis began making medical students wash their hands, and the death rate in his Vienna maternity clinic fell ninefold. Two years later he was out of a job. Jeffrey Pfeffer of Stanford argues that doing good work and getting it adopted are separate skills, and that the second one can be learned.

Chen-Yang Su · 21 October 2026 ·  

Main takeaways
  • Semmelweis’s handwashing cut deaths in his clinic from 11.4% of births in 1846 to 1.3% in 1848. Two scholars who recalculated the numbers trace the opposition he met to something other than doubts about the washing.
  • Pfeffer writes that power is important for career success, “maybe more important than job performance,” and he calls the skills behind it “all learnable and teachable.”
  • Students who had to get five strangers to fill out a questionnaire expected to ask 20.5 people and needed 10.5.
  • In a map test done in pairs, overconfidence predicted how competent a partner rated someone about as well as actual skill did.
  • A random donation to new Kickstarter projects raised the share that drew other backers from 39% to 70%.

In May 1847, Ignaz Semmelweis, an assistant in the First Maternity Clinic of the Vienna General Hospital, began requiring medical students to wash their hands in chloride of lime before they examined women giving birth. The students came to the ward from the dissecting room. Semmelweis suspected that they carried what he later called “decomposing animal organic matter” from the corpses on their hands.

The First Clinic trained doctors and medical students. The hospital’s Second Clinic trained midwives, who did no post-mortems, and far fewer of its mothers died. In 1846, 459 of the 4,010 women who gave birth in the First Clinic died, about one in nine. In 1848, the first full year of washing, 45 of 3,556 died, about one in eighty. That was almost exactly the rate in the midwives’ clinic.

Two years later Semmelweis was out of a job. In 1849 he was removed from his post as assistant. When Pablo Jadraque and K. Codell Carter, who translated Semmelweis’s book into English, recalculated the clinic’s deaths in 2017, they found that the rate stayed low after he left, which suggests that his successors kept up the washing. The opposition he met, they concluded, “had other sources than doubts about the effectiveness of the chlorine washings.”

Semmelweis waited thirteen years to publish his own account. The historian Irvine Loudon describes a treatise of more than 500 pages that mixes “passages of great clarity” with “bellicose passages in which he attacks his critics.” In 1865 Semmelweis was admitted to an asylum, where he died two weeks later. His work, Loudon writes, “had very little effect on obstetric practice.”

Semmelweis had the numbers. He could not get his profession to act on them. Jeffrey Pfeffer has spent his career studying that gap between being right and getting something done.

The cod liver oil of the business school

Pfeffer is the Thomas D. Dee II Professor of Organizational Behavior at Stanford Graduate School of Business, where he has taught since 1979, and he holds a courtesy appointment in health policy at Stanford’s medical school. After joining Stanford he wanted to teach something “important, under-researched, and under-taught,” and he chose power. His elective, now called The Paths to Power, carries a boldface warning on its syllabus, “This Class Is Not for Everyone.” In the winter of 2021, two students were turned away for every one admitted. A student once described it as “the cod liver oil of the GSB: You know it’s good for you, but you feel a little nervous about it.”

Pfeffer made his case to general readers in two books, Power: Why Some People Have It and Others Don’t (2010) and 7 Rules of Power (2022). Power, he writes, is important for career success, “maybe more important than job performance.” Stanford’s summary of the first book describes the mistake he wants to correct. “By believing that life is fair, we tend to subscribe to the ‘just-world phenomenon,’ which leaves us unprepared for the challenges and competition of the real world.”

The rules are blunt, and Pfeffer’s central claim about them is hopeful. “This stuff is all learnable and teachable,” he said in the 2022 interview. “You’re not born one thing or the other.”

Power in Pfeffer’s sense is the capacity to get things done. Formal authority is one source of it. Control of something other people need is another. A hypothetical postdoc who maintains the lab’s only working copy of a hard-won dataset becomes central to every collaboration that needs it, whatever their title.

Being right is half the job

Pfeffer’s first target is the belief that good work speaks for itself. People who decide on promotions or grants rarely have time to judge every contribution in depth, so they lean on quicker signals, such as a recommendation from someone they trust.

Two hypothetical engineers solve equally hard problems in the same year. One of them also explains her work in the meetings where leaders decide what to fund next. When a team-lead role opens, the people choosing have heard of only one of them.

The just-world belief makes that outcome look fair after the fact. If merit is always rewarded, the engineer who was passed over must have had less of it. Pfeffer treats making work visible as part of the work, and there is nothing virtuous about staying invisible.

Semmelweis is the extreme case. His method apparently survived in his own clinic after he left. Elsewhere it had few supporters, and antisepsis did not become routine in obstetrics until the 1880s.

Asking works twice as often as people expect

Pfeffer’s first two rules, get out of your own way and break the rules, are about inhibitions people learn early. “Most people will follow the rules, because that’s what they’re taught to do,” he said. “In order to acquire power, you have to be brave, which is why most people don’t acquire power.”

One of those inhibitions is the expectation that strangers will say no. Francis Flynn, now a colleague of Pfeffer’s at Stanford, tested how accurate that expectation is. In a 2008 study with Vanessa Bohns, 42 Columbia University students each had to get five strangers to fill out a questionnaire, using a single scripted line, “Will you fill out a questionnaire?” The students asked to predict beforehand expected to approach 20.5 people on average. They needed 10.5.

Bohns later reviewed a run of such studies in which participants made requests of more than 14,000 strangers. On average they underestimated how often people would agree by 48%. The error, she found, comes from forgetting how awkward it is to say no.

Pfeffer’s classroom guests tell the same story from experience. Christina Troitino, a former student in his course, told one class how she talked her way into dinner with Martha Stewart. “I’m not the loudest person in the room,” she said, “but I’m shameless.”

Who starts on the 50-yard line

In the same class, a student objected, “That’s all well and good if you’re a tall, good-looking white guy.” Pfeffer agrees that people start from different places. “People from underrepresented groups don’t start off on the 50-yard line,” he said in 2022, which is why he thinks they need these skills more. The penalties are measurable. In Laurie Rudman’s 1998 experiments, women who promoted themselves were rated as more competent but less likable. They were also rated as less hireable, except by men whose own payoff depended on the women’s performance. Pfeffer’s rules work inside that bias and leave it in place.

Conflict comes with the job

In Power, Pfeffer lists seven personal qualities that help people gain influence, and he sorts them into will and skill. Two of them pull against each other. Empathy lets a person see a dispute the way the other side sees it. The capacity to tolerate conflict lets them keep pushing after the other side says no.

Many capable people avoid disagreement because they want to be seen as easy to work with. Pfeffer is unsympathetic. When a student asked where assertive ends and “just plain annoying” begins, he replied, “You could be called much worse things than annoying.” In the same class he called likability “not really relevant.”

Semmelweis had the second quality in abundance and little of the first. Loudon calls him “a complex, difficult, and dogmatic man, intolerant to the point of paranoia of the slightest criticism.” His critics were the obstetricians he needed to convince, and his treatise spends page after page attacking them. Pfeffer’s list asks for both qualities at once.

Reputation arrives before you do

Reputation matters most when people must decide before they can judge. Investors back founders before a product works, and hiring committees choose people whose next decade they cannot see. Both rely on what they have heard.

In 1968 the sociologist Robert Merton described what this does to credit in science. Working from Harriet Zuckerman’s interviews with Nobel laureates, he found that the same contribution earns more recognition when an eminent scientist makes it. He called this the Matthew effect, after the verse “For unto every one that hath shall be given.” One laureate in physics put it plainly. “The world is peculiar in this matter of how it gives credit. It tends to give the credit to [already] famous people.”

Pfeffer’s fourth rule, create a powerful brand, runs on the same mechanism. He calls most organizational processes “self-reinforcing.” “If you’ve claimed competence, in fact, that just causes more people to want to work with you, and you are able to attract more talent,” he said. “So you become more competent.”

His third rule, show up in powerful fashion, extends the argument to how people present themselves. “We live in a world where it’s almost impossible to find out the truth,” he said. “Therefore, appearance is always reality.” He once met the head of business intelligence at a large accounting firm and told a friend afterwards that the man seemed to have none. The friend replied, “He looks good in a suit.”

The research backs him uncomfortably well. In a 2012 study led by Cameron Anderson at Berkeley, 76 students each placed 15 US cities on a blank map and rated how they had done against everyone else. Then they did the task again in pairs. Overconfidence predicted how competent a partner rated someone (r = .36) about as well as actual skill did (r = .39). In a follow-up, group members did not punish people even after learning that they had been overconfident.

That is a fact about observers, and organizations pay for it when they promote people who are confidently wrong. A scientist can state the limits of a result plainly and still speak with authority about what it shows. Doubt about the evidence is part of the job. Doubt about whether one belongs in the conversation is a different thing.

Good ideas cross between groups

Pfeffer’s fifth rule is to network relentlessly, and his reasoning is blunt. “People to whom you’re strongly tied tend to know the same people, and have the same information,” he said. “So what the hell good are they? You need non-redundant people and information.”

He was pointing to research from 1973, when Mark Granovetter argued that acquaintances matter because they reach circles your close friends do not. In 2022 a randomized experiment on more than 20 million LinkedIn users found that moderately weak ties, counted by mutual connections, produced the most job moves, as I described in How to get luckier. Weak ties helped more in digital industries, and strong ties helped more in less digital ones.

Structural holes

The sociologist Ronald Burt’s term for the gaps between groups whose members rarely talk to one another. Opinion and behavior are more alike within a group than between groups, so a person with contacts on both sides of a gap hears ideas that neither side has. Burt calls that person a broker.

Burt measured what brokers do for ideas. He studied 673 managers who ran the supply chain of one of America’s largest electronics companies in 2001 and asked them for their best idea to improve it. Two senior executives rated the ideas. Managers whose discussion partners spanned separate groups proposed ideas the executives judged more valuable, and they were paid more than peers in comparable jobs. Burt called it “creativity as an import-export business.”

The value in Burt’s data came from what crossed the gap, an idea routine on one side and new on the other. Elsewhere in the paper he put it more starkly. “An idea is as valuable as an audience is willing to credit it with being.”

Success compounds, up to a point

Pfeffer’s sixth rule is to use your power, and he grounds it in something close to physics. “If the world could change without the exercise of influence, it already would have changed,” he said. “Things are in equilibrium, and if you want to get them out of equilibrium, you need some force.” Using power also tends to produce more of it. “Most people who have power use it to get more power and to keep their power.”

Some of that compounding needs no merit at all. In field experiments published in 2014, Arnout van de Rijt and colleagues handed early successes to randomly chosen recipients on four websites, then recorded what strangers did next. On Kickstarter they donated to 100 of 200 new projects, picked at random. Among the projects they skipped, 39% went on to attract other backers. Among the projects they funded, 70% did. The same pattern held on the other three sites.

The experiments also found a limit. Bigger head starts produced little extra advantage, and the authors concluded that cumulative advantage plays “a more modest role” in inequality “than previously thought.”

Pfeffer’s seventh rule describes what success does to memory. Once someone has acquired power, “what you did to get it will be forgiven, forgotten, or both.” People are drawn to money and power, he explained, and “they will engage in rationalizations so they can be close to what they desire to be close to.”

The rule describes how observers judge winners. It is a poor recipe for winning. Stories about founders who ignored their critics come from founders who succeeded, and the many who ignored their critics and failed do not get profiles. Even Stanford’s summary of Power warns that advice from people in charge “often puts a rosy spin on their ascent.”

Where rule seven runs out

Pfeffer’s example of power entrenching itself is the super-majority voting share, which lets founders keep control whatever other investors want. Elizabeth Holmes held such shares in Theranos. In 2018 the SEC charged her with raising more than $700 million through “an elaborate, years-long fraud,” and she gave up her voting control to settle. In 2022 a federal jury convicted her of defrauding investors. The SEC had stated the limit in 2018. There is “no exemption from the anti-fraud provisions of the federal securities laws,” it said, for a company that is “the subject of exuberant media attention.”

A tool for any purpose

Pfeffer insists that power is neutral. “Power is a tool that can be used for any different purpose to which you want to apply it,” he said. “One should not confuse the value of the tool with how it’s being used.”

That neutrality is the strongest objection to his books. Advice to cultivate useful relationships and project confidence serves a manipulator as well as a reformer. A manager can gain influence by intimidating subordinates and then stop hearing bad news from them. A researcher can win attention by overstating a result and spend the trust that makes results worth reading.

Declining to learn how power works leaves it in other hands. Decisions about funding and hiring are made by people with influence, and those who understand how such decisions get made are better placed to argue for good projects. Power expands what a person can do. Whether anyone else benefits depends on what it is used for.

How the mothers were saved

Semmelweis’s idea did reach the maternity wards, by a road he never saw. In Glasgow, the surgeon Joseph Lister read Louis Pasteur’s papers on putrefaction on the advice of Thomas Anderson, a professor of chemistry, and reasoned that the process Pasteur had found in fermentation was also at work in infected wounds. From 1867 he published a series of papers in The Lancet and the British Medical Journal on dressing wounds with carbolic acid.

His statistics were thinner than his legend. Lister’s best-known numbers, 16 deaths in 35 amputations before antisepsis and 6 in 40 after, came from his own wards, and the historian Ulrich Tröhler counts more than a decade of fighting among British surgeons over whether the method worked. Lister kept publishing. In 1877 he moved to King’s College Hospital in London, and two years later the city’s leading surgeons were still waiting for proof. When the surgeon Lawson Tait was still demanding statistics in 1890, Lister, who “did not relish controversy,” politely ignored him.

By the 1880s antisepsis had spread from surgery into maternity hospitals, and within a few years, Loudon writes, mortality there “fell dramatically.” Loudon traces antisepsis in obstetrics to Lister’s surgical methods, which reached the lying-in wards long after Semmelweis’s death. Lister had not even heard of Semmelweis until long after his own method was firmly established.

Semmelweis’s reputation came back after 1887, and by the 1920s, Loudon writes, his story “had all the elements of a Hollywood epic.” It is usually told as a lesson about stubborn doctors. Pfeffer’s books suggest a second lesson. Semmelweis had thousands of births in his tables. Lister had 75 amputations and a stack of case reports, and he spent more than twenty years getting surgeons to use his method.

Doing good work and getting it adopted are separate skills, and the second one can be learned. Semmelweis had the first. Mothers who gave birth in hospitals in the 1880s owed much to a surgeon who also had the second, and who spent his influence on something worth spreading.
