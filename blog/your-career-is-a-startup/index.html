Your career is a startup, not a ladder

Reid Hoffman set out to become a philosophy professor. He co-founded LinkedIn instead, after a product job at Apple and a startup of his own that failed. In The Start-Up of You, he and Ben Casnocha argue that a career works like a startup that never ships a final version, and that the backup plan has to exist before the first plan breaks.

Chen-Yang Su · 29 October 2026 ·  

Main takeaways
  • Hoffman and Casnocha call a career that stays open to revision permanent beta. Hoffman’s own plan moved from academia to product management to founding companies, and each step grew out of the one before.
  • They describe a competitive advantage as three gears that only turn together. Your assets are one gear and your aspirations a second. The third is what the market needs.
  • In Mark Granovetter’s survey of 54 people who found a job through a personal contact, only 9 had seen that contact often. The other 45 heard from people they saw occasionally or rarely.
  • Before putting his savings into his first startup, Hoffman asked his father whether he could move into the guest bedroom if it failed. That was his Plan Z. His Plan B, PayPal, grew out of walks with Peter Thiel and Max Levchin.
  • Casnocha judges a risk by its worst case. He is open to a risk whose worst case he could survive, and wary of one that could leave him bankrupt with his reputation destroyed.

In the summer of 2004, Sean Parker was raising money for Facebook, then a website for college students run by the 20-year-old Mark Zuckerberg. Parker later wrote in a tribute in TIME that Reid Hoffman was the first person they called. Hoffman had launched LinkedIn the year before. He was impressed by Facebook, and he also judged that leading its first investment while running another social network would be a conflict, according to his profile at the Academy of Achievement. He arranged a meeting with his friend Peter Thiel instead. Thiel agreed to lend Facebook $500,000, which would convert into a 10.2% stake.

Hoffman and Thiel knew each other from Stanford. In the New York Review of Books, Anne Diebel describes the undergraduate Thiel, who “stayed up late sparring with Reid Hoffman, a progressive from Berkeley he had met in a philosophy class.” Hoffman later called his choice to step back from Facebook’s round “the most expensive decision” of his business career. Thiel’s half-million dollars reached Facebook through a friendship that began in a philosophy class.

My grandfather recently sent me two messages on LINE about Hoffman. One listed three ways Hoffman says a successful career gets built. The other said to manage a career the way a founder manages a startup. Both ideas come from The Start-Up of You, the book Hoffman wrote with Ben Casnocha in 2012. I started at Stanford this month, where Hoffman and Thiel once argued about philosophy, and reading the book here, I kept finding its best examples in Hoffman’s own career.

There is no final version

Software companies release beta versions of products they expect to keep changing once people use them. Hoffman and Casnocha borrow the idea for careers.

Permanent beta

Permanent beta is Hoffman and Casnocha’s name for treating a career as a product that is never finished. In a podcast conversation about the book, Hoffman describes it as being “an infinite learner.” The direction can hold for years while the route changes with what each step reveals.

Hoffman’s route changed several times. He graduated from Stanford in 1990 in symbolic systems, a program that combines philosophy with computer science, and earned a master’s in philosophy at Oxford in 1993. He meant to become a professor and a public intellectual. At Oxford he concluded that academic philosophy would reach too few people, and he went looking for “a more direct impact on society,” in the words of his Academy of Achievement profile. In 1994 he joined Apple to work on eWorld, an early online service.

Each version kept something from the one before. At Apple he tested product management while still in his old job, and two product jobs came before he co-founded SocialNet in 1997. When SocialNet stalled, what he had learned from it was what Thiel and Max Levchin wanted on the board of the company that became PayPal. Each move in Hoffman’s career was an experiment run from the one before, with a fallback in place before he needed it.

Permanent beta has a failure mode of its own. A startup that rebuilds its product after every complaint never ships anything. Hoffman stayed with each plan long enough for it to produce evidence, three years in SocialNet’s case.

Three gears have to turn together

In a podcast episode on the book, Casnocha and Hoffman describe “the three gears to competitive advantage.” Casnocha’s warning about them is short. “Any one of them is insufficient.”

The first gear is your assets. Hard assets are things like “the money in your bank account.” Soft assets are, in Casnocha’s words, “your skills, your network, your personal brand,” and Hoffman adds that “soft assets tend to be the things that will compound better.” The second gear is your aspirations and values, the work you want to be doing in ten or twenty years. The third is market realities, meaning where the demand is and who else is competing to meet it.

Casnocha’s example of a missing gear is nursing. California has “a huge demand for nurses,” so the market gear turns, and a person who hates nursing will probably not last in the job. The reverse failure is a passion that nobody will pay for. Sara Blakely is one of their examples of the gears meshing. She spent years selling fax machines door to door, and the selling became an asset when she started Spanx.

A hypothetical biology student who has also learned to program holds two assets that are common on their own. Together they can let the student automate a lab’s image analysis. A lab that needs that done may choose this student over a stronger biologist or a stronger programmer. The test is whether the student solves one group’s problem better than the other options that group has.

Job leads travel through acquaintances

Hoffman’s introduction worked because he and Thiel were close friends. Most career information moves through looser links. In 1973, Mark Granovetter, now a sociologist at Stanford, published The strength of weak ties. For his doctoral research he had surveyed recent white-collar job changers in a Boston suburb. He asked those who found the job through a contact how often they had seen that person around the time the tip came.

Only 9 of the 54 had seen their contact often, meaning at least twice a week. Thirty had seen the contact occasionally, and 15 once a year or less. The contact was often “an old college friend or a former workmate or employer,” someone kept up with only sporadically. “Chance meetings or mutual friends operated to reactivate such ties,” Granovetter wrote. His explanation was that close friends move in the same circles you do, so they tend to hear about the same openings. An acquaintance moves in a different circle and hears about different ones. He called these looser connections weak ties.

In 2022, an experiment on more than 20 million LinkedIn members tested the idea at scale, as I described in How to get luckier. Ties with a moderate number of mutual connections led to the most new jobs, and MIT News put the turning point at about 10 mutual connections.

What the weak-ties evidence covers

Granovetter’s figures come from 54 people in one Boston suburb around 1970, and his “often” group is only nine people. The LinkedIn study is far larger and randomized, and it covers one platform, where a new connection costs a click. Both describe the routes that job information takes. Neither shows that collecting acquaintances in bulk improves a career.

Plan A, Plan B and Plan Z

Hoffman and Casnocha’s planning method has three parts. In a podcast episode called “Planning to Adapt,” Casnocha defines Plan A as “what you’re doing right now.” Plan B is where you might pivot if something changes, ideally close enough to Plan A that your skills carry over. Plan Z is “the plan that you do if everything else falls apart.”

In 1997, Hoffman’s Plan A was, in his words, “Make SocialNet successful.” He was about to put his savings into the company. Before he did, he asked his father whether he could move into the guest bedroom if SocialNet failed and left him broke. He promised to get a job and pay rent until he could move out. His father said yes. “I think it’s good that you’re taking risk in order to make something happen,” Hoffman recalls him saying. “I like seeing you, so sure.”

His Plan B came from conversations. He “started going on walks with Peter Thiel and Max Levchin,” he says, “and kind of telling them what I’d learned.” They asked him to join the board of the company they were putting together, which became PayPal. By 2000, SocialNet was clearly not going to become what Hoffman wanted. He shut it down, returned his investors’ original capital and joined PayPal full-time as chief operating officer.

A Plan Z depends on who you are

Hoffman’s Plan Z was a parent with a spare room who was willing to offer it. Casnocha’s examples of a Plan Z are a spare room or savings to draw on. Many students have neither, and some are the person their family relies on. A thinner safety net makes the same bet more dangerous, so the size of a sensible risk differs from one person to the next.

Risks you can recover from

Putting your savings into a startup sounds reckless. The guest bedroom changed what was at stake. If SocialNet failed, Hoffman would lose money and keep a place to live while he started again. He would also have learned how an online network grows, and he co-founded LinkedIn in December 2002 with two former SocialNet colleagues.

Casnocha described the test behind a bet like this in a 2013 interview. It starts from the worst outcome. “If I feel like the worst-case scenario is my company goes under, I go bankrupt and my reputation is destroyed,” he said, then he is wary of the risk. The risks that are hard to reverse share one feature. Their worst case damages something slow to rebuild, such as a credit record or other people’s trust.

The most interesting version of the job

One line in my grandfather’s messages was about ambition. He wrote that I should picture the most interesting version of what I am doing and work toward it.

At Apple, Hoffman worked in user experience, and he suspected product management would suit him better. The most interesting version of his job was one he did not have yet. He wrote sample product requirement documents on his own and took them to the director of product management, James Isaacs. As Hoffman tells it in “Planning to Adapt”, he asked Isaacs, “will you assemble a group and review and give me feedback on them?”

Isaacs liked the work. He became Hoffman’s reference for a product-management job at Fujitsu, the last job Hoffman held before he started SocialNet in 1997.
