Careers

Choosing the game, not just the outcome

A high salary pays well for your time. I’m more interested in owning something whose value can grow beyond it.

Chen-Yang Su · 9 October 2026 ·  

Main takeaways
  • A high salary and a high-leverage career are not the same thing. The difference is how closely your earnings are tied to your own time.
  • Ownership lets you capture value from something that scales beyond your own labor. But uncapped upside does not promise a good financial outcome.
  • A career choice is not only a bet on expected income. It also sets how much autonomy, uncertainty, and responsibility you carry, and which problems you spend your time on.
  • Being recognized as exceptional is different from building something exceptionally valuable.
  • I don’t want to start a company for its own sake. I want to find a problem worth solving first, then a way of solving it that can grow beyond what one person could do alone.

I recently looked up how much pediatricians earn compared with other medical specialties. According to Doximity’s 2026 Physician Compensation Report, general pediatricians in the United States earned about $274,000 on average in 2025.

That’s a lot of money by almost any standard, and it takes years of medical school and residency to earn. Pediatricians have expertise few people have, their work matters to patients and families, and their careers are far more predictable than a startup founder’s. By most measures, medicine is an excellent career.

Yet the more I thought about it, the less a high salary looked like what I wanted. The amount of money wasn’t the problem. The structure of the career was.

A physician’s pay grows with experience, specialty, and practice setting. But a physician who mainly sees patients is limited by time. There are only so many patients one person can see in a day.

Physicians can also own practices, develop medical technologies, and invest, so the line I care about isn’t between doctors and entrepreneurs. It’s between earning mainly through your own labor and owning something that creates value beyond it.

That made me ask a larger question.

What if the most predictable path to success leads to a kind of success you don’t actually want?

Income and wealth are not the same thing

Naval Ravikant’s How to Get Rich (without getting lucky) shaped how I think about this. Naval separates earning money by renting out your time from building wealth through ownership and leverage.

Imagine two people. The first earns $300,000 a year providing a professional service. The second owns a large stake in a software company whose product is used by thousands of organizations.

In any given year the first person may well earn more. They may also have more financial security, and they can build real wealth by saving and investing. But their professional income still depends on their continuing to work.

The second person’s company can earn revenue from thousands of customers without the founder serving each one. What the founder ends up with depends on what the business is worth, how much of it they own, and whether they can ever turn that stake into money.

This is leverage. The same hour of work can serve one customer or thousands.

Software is the clearest case, because serving one more user costs almost nothing. Code written once can run millions of times, and a small team can build something used around the world. Capital, teams, intellectual property, and media can do the same.

The key idea

Leverage lets output grow faster than effort. Ownership decides how much of that output comes back to you. Creating value and capturing value are two different problems.

An employee can do much of the work that makes a company succeed and be paid only a salary. An investor can own a stake that grows in value without working there at all. That says nothing about whose work matters more. They have different contracts.

I’ve become more interested in building things whose value isn’t tied to the hours I personally work. That isn’t because I dislike work. If I’m going to put years of energy into something, I want the result to be able to grow beyond what I could do alone.

The appeal of an uncapped game

I’ve always wanted to do something exceptional. Part of that is competitiveness. I like setting ambitious goals and finding ways to stand out.

But being exceptional inside an established system is different from building something that changes the system.

In many professions the path is well defined. You finish the training, meet the standards, and move up a hierarchy toward more responsibility and pay. There is plenty of room for excellence on that path, and a great physician, scientist, or teacher can matter far more than their salary suggests. But the incentives and the rungs of the ladder were set by institutions long before you arrived.

Starting a company works differently. There is no residency for founders and no promotion schedule, and no institution decides whether you’re qualified to try. You find a problem, build a solution, and try to convince people it’s worth paying for.

In principle the upside is uncapped. You might build something that serves a hundred people, or a million, or nobody at all.

Uncapped is not the same as a good bet

Once you account for the chance of failure, dilution, and years of forgone salary, a startup with enormous upside can still have a poor expected financial return. We also hear mostly about the founders who succeeded. Companies that never find customers or never raise money rarely get written about.

So I can’t claim that starting a company pays better than a conventional career. For many people, it doesn’t.

The better argument is that people can rationally prefer different distributions of outcomes. Some want a stable career with a clear trajectory. Others will accept much more uncertainty for a chance at an extraordinary result. Neither preference is wrong, but they lead to very different careers. I’m drawn to the second.

Autonomy is another form of compensation

Salary comparisons also miss the other reason I’m drawn to starting something, which is freedom. I want to choose which problems I work on, move quickly when I see an opportunity, and try ideas without waiting for an institution to approve them.

Academic research is shaped by funding, peer review, and what gets published. Medicine is governed by hospital systems, regulation, and standards of care, many of which exist to protect patients. Employees at large companies work within a strategy and a reporting line that someone else set.

Starting a company doesn’t remove constraints. Founders answer to customers, employees, investors, and regulators, and in the end to whether anyone pays. A founder who can’t find customers is no freer of outside judgment than a researcher waiting on a grant review. In some ways a founder carries more obligations than an employee. Employees and customers depend on the founder’s decisions, and investors often hold contractual rights over the major ones.

So the freedom isn’t absolute. The difference is that you get more say in which constraints you take on.

You decide which problem is worth pursuing, try different solutions, recruit people who believe in it, and change direction when customers tell you it isn’t working. The responsibility is large, and so is the agency. For me, the agency is part of the reward.

The danger of chasing prestige instead of value

Ambition has an uncomfortable side that I’ve been thinking about. It’s easy to confuse wanting to build something extraordinary with wanting to be seen as extraordinary. From the outside, the two look the same.

A degree from a famous university, a paper in an elite journal, a startup that gets press. Each can be a real achievement, and each can become a stand-in for one. Recognition is not the same as contribution. An academic can be highly cited for work that changes little in practice. A startup can raise a lot of money and get plenty of press without ever becoming a sustainable business.

The reverse happens too. Someone can spend years on an obscure but essential technology that improves thousands of lives and never be publicly recognized.

If I had to choose between a little-known company that improves people’s lives and wide recognition for work that matters less, I would choose the company. I do care about achievement, recognition, and wealth. I want them to follow from building something valuable, not to replace it.

The goal is to create exceptional value, not to look exceptional.

Economic value and social value also don’t always line up. A product can sell well without making anyone better off, and some of the most important problems in healthcare and science are hard to make money from. So financial upside can’t be my only test for what to build. It has to be weighed against what kind of value I would be creating.

Why this matters to me as a scientist

I trained in human genetics and computational biology, and my work is increasingly in AI. I’ve spent years developing computational methods for biological questions, in the hope that the results would eventually improve human health.

Research has also shown me how much work sits between a discovery and anything that changes a patient’s care. A promising result in a paper is not a diagnostic test. A model that scores well on a benchmark has not yet improved anyone’s treatment.

Those gaps are hard problems, and they are also opportunities. Someone who understands both the science and what it takes to deploy it in a clinic could build something valuable. That takes different work from research. You have to learn what people need, who would use the product, who would pay for it, what they use now, and why they would switch.

That’s the work I want to try. Starting a company isn’t more impressive than doing science. I want to find out whether what I know can become something that scales, and I want to help carry useful knowledge out of papers and into practice.

Ownership without a valuable problem is meaningless

An obsession with leverage can put things in the wrong order. You start by wanting to own a large company, then go looking for something to build.

A company isn’t valuable because it has a founder, a product, and investors. It’s valuable when it solves a problem someone cares about, in a way that pays for itself. Half of a company nobody wants is worth very little. A small stake in a lasting business can be worth a great deal. So the question isn’t how much equity I can own. It’s what valuable thing I can help build, and what ownership makes sense for building it.

This has changed how I think about my next step. It isn’t necessarily to start a company as fast as possible. I would rather find a problem I believe is important, understand it unusually well, and test whether it can be solved as a business. That means talking to clinicians, watching how they work, building prototypes, checking whether anyone would pay, and sometimes learning that an idea I loved doesn’t work.

I don’t want to spend years defending a hypothesis just because it was mine. If the evidence changes, I want to change with it.

In a previous essay I wrote about optimism in the face of uncertainty, and about choosing experiments for what they teach you. The same rule applies here.

The rule

Find the problem before the company. Don’t make the biggest bet first. Run experiments where the downside is survivable, the upside is large, and the result teaches you something, and commit more as the evidence comes in.

For now, finding a problem worth working on matters more to me than the title of founder.

Choosing the game

No career is right for everyone. The path that feels confining to one person gives another exactly the stability and meaning they want. Starting a company, whatever its upside, is a bad choice for someone who values predictability or doesn’t want to run a business.

It isn’t the only way to build wealth either. A high salary invested steadily over decades can buy real financial independence, and many professionals do enormous good through their work without owning a company.

But a career is more than a bet on expected income. It’s how you spend a large part of your life.

Questions worth asking
  • Which problems do you want to spend your time on?
  • How much uncertainty and responsibility are you willing to carry?
  • How much does autonomy matter to you?
  • What outcome would make the effort worth it?

For me, ownership, autonomy, and the chance of outsized impact matter enough that I’m willing to try a less predictable path. I don’t know that I’ll succeed. I would still rather test it than rule it out because a safer career is available.

A high salary buys comfort, security, and eventually freedom, and those are worth a lot. What I want is to build something whose value isn’t capped by a pay scale, can grow beyond what I contribute myself, and could give me the freedom and resources to take on harder problems.

I don’t just want to optimize the outcome of my career. I want to choose a game where I can experiment, build, and take real risks on something bigger than myself. The game we choose shapes what we might achieve, and it also shapes who we become.
