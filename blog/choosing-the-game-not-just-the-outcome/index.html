Careers

Choosing the game, not just the outcome

On the difference between earning a good living, creating wealth, and choosing a career with uncapped possibilities.

Chen-Yang Su · 9 October 2026 ·  

Main takeaways
  • A high salary and a high-leverage career are not necessarily the same thing. The difference lies in how closely economic rewards are tied to an individual’s time.
  • Ownership creates the possibility of capturing value from something that scales beyond personal labor. But unlimited theoretical upside does not guarantee a favorable financial outcome.
  • Career choices are not simply about maximizing expected income. They also reflect our preferences for autonomy, uncertainty, responsibility, and the kinds of problems we want to solve.
  • Prestige and achievement are not interchangeable with impact. Being recognized as exceptional is different from creating something exceptionally valuable.
  • The goal is not to start a company for its own sake. It is to find a problem worth solving and a way to create value that can grow beyond what one person could accomplish alone.

I recently found myself looking into how much pediatricians earn compared with other medical specialties.

According to Doximity’s 2026 Physician Compensation Report, general pediatricians in the United States earned approximately $274,000 on average in 2025.

That’s a substantial income by almost any conventional standard.

Becoming a pediatrician requires years of demanding education and training. Physicians develop expertise that few people possess, perform work that matters enormously to patients and families, and generally have a much more predictable career trajectory than someone attempting to build a startup.

By most conventional measures, medicine is an excellent career.

And yet, the more I thought about it, the more I realized that even a very high salary wasn’t necessarily what I wanted.

It wasn’t really about the amount of money.

It was about the structure of the career itself.

A physician might spend years completing medical school and residency, eventually earning an excellent income. Their compensation can grow with experience, specialty, practice setting, and other factors. But for a physician primarily providing clinical services, the amount of work they can personally perform is still constrained by time.

There are only so many patients one person can see and so many hours in a day.

Of course, physicians can also build businesses, own practices, develop medical technologies, and invest. The distinction isn’t between doctors and entrepreneurs. It’s between earning primarily through one’s labor and owning something capable of creating value beyond that labor.

And that distinction made me ask a larger question.

What if the career that offers the most predictable path to success isn’t the career that offers the kind of success you actually want?

Income and wealth are not the same thing

One of the ideas that has influenced my thinking comes from Naval Ravikant’s How to Get Rich (without getting lucky).

Naval distinguishes between earning money by exchanging time for compensation and building wealth through ownership and leverage.

The distinction is simple, but its implications are enormous.

Imagine two people.

The first earns $300,000 per year providing a valuable professional service.

The second owns a meaningful stake in a software company whose product is used by thousands of organizations.

The first person might earn considerably more in a given year. They might also enjoy greater financial stability and accumulate substantial wealth by saving and investing their income.

But their professional earnings remain relatively dependent on their continued labor.

The second person’s company might generate revenue from thousands of customers without requiring the founder to personally serve each one.

The founder’s financial outcome depends on the value of the business, their ownership stake, and whether that value can ultimately be realized.

This is leverage.

A single unit of effort can produce value that scales far beyond the individual who performed it.

Software is an especially interesting example because the marginal cost of distributing an existing product to another user can be relatively low. A piece of code can run millions of times. A digital product can serve customers around the world. A small team can sometimes create something with an enormous reach.

But leverage is not limited to software. It also exists through capital, organizations, intellectual property, media, and distribution.

The fundamental idea is that the relationship between personal effort and economic output need not be linear.

And ownership determines how much of that economic value ultimately accrues to the person who helped create it.

Creating value and capturing value are two different problems.

Someone can make an enormous contribution to a company while receiving only a salary. Someone else might own an asset that appreciates dramatically without directly contributing an equivalent number of working hours.

That doesn’t make one person’s work more meaningful than the other’s. It simply reflects different economic arrangements.

I’ve become increasingly interested in building things where the value created is not tightly coupled to the number of hours I personally work.

Not because I dislike working.

Quite the opposite.

If I’m going to dedicate an enormous amount of energy to something, I want the possibility that the result can grow far beyond what I could accomplish alone.

The appeal of an uncapped game

I’ve always been attracted to the idea of doing something exceptional.

Part of that comes from being competitive. I enjoy setting ambitious goals, challenging myself, and finding ways to differentiate what I do.

But I’ve increasingly realized that there’s an important distinction between being exceptional within an established system and creating something that changes the system itself.

In many professions, the path is relatively well defined.

You complete the necessary training, meet established standards, advance through a hierarchy, and eventually reach positions of greater responsibility and compensation.

There is considerable room for excellence within these structures. An extraordinary physician, scientist, or teacher can have an impact that far exceeds what any salary conveys.

Still, the incentives and progression are often shaped by institutions that existed long before you arrived.

Entrepreneurship offers a different possibility.

There is no standardized residency for starting a company. No predetermined sequence of promotions. No single institution that decides whether you’re qualified to attempt something ambitious.

You can identify a problem, develop a solution, and attempt to persuade the world that what you’ve created is valuable.

In principle, the upside is uncapped.

You might build something that serves a hundred people.

Or a million.

Or nobody at all.

That last possibility is important.

An uncapped opportunity is not the same as an attractive investment. A startup with enormous potential upside may still have a terrible expected financial outcome once the probability of failure, opportunity cost, dilution, and years of forgone income are considered.

We also tend to hear disproportionately about successful founders. The companies that never find customers, never raise funding, or quietly disappear receive much less attention.

So the argument cannot simply be that entrepreneurship offers a higher financial return than a conventional career.

For many people, it won’t.

The more interesting argument is that different people may rationally prefer different distributions of possible outcomes.

Some people value predictability, stability, and a clear professional trajectory.

Others are willing to accept considerably more uncertainty for the possibility of an extraordinary outcome.

Neither preference is inherently superior.

But they can lead to very different career choices.

And I’ve realized that I’m strongly drawn to the latter.

Autonomy is another form of compensation

There’s another reason entrepreneurship appeals to me that isn’t captured by salary comparisons.

Freedom.

I like the idea of being able to choose which problems to work on, move quickly when I see an opportunity, and pursue ideas without constantly waiting for institutional approval.

In academia, research is shaped by funding, peer review, publication incentives, and institutional priorities.

In medicine, training and clinical practice are governed by professional requirements, hospital systems, regulations, and standards of care. Many of these constraints are essential for protecting patients.

In corporations, employees must operate within organizational strategies, reporting structures, and business priorities.

Every system has constraints.

Entrepreneurship doesn’t eliminate them.

Founders answer to customers, employees, investors, regulators, and ultimately economic reality. A founder who cannot attract customers is no more independent of external judgment than a researcher whose funding depends on peer review.

In some ways, founding a company can create even more obligations than employment. Employees and customers may depend on decisions you make, while investors may have contractual rights over major company decisions.

The freedom isn’t absolute.

But there is still an important difference.

You have greater freedom to choose which constraints you want to take on.

You can decide what problem is worth pursuing. You can experiment with different solutions. You can recruit people who share your conviction. And if the market tells you that your approach isn’t working, you can change direction.

The responsibility is enormous.

So is the agency.

For me, that agency is part of the reward.

The danger of chasing prestige instead of value

There is, however, something uncomfortable about ambition that I’ve been thinking about.

It’s easy to confuse wanting to create something extraordinary with wanting to be perceived as extraordinary.

The two motivations can look identical from the outside.

You might pursue a prestigious degree, join a famous institution, publish in an elite journal, or start a company that attracts considerable attention.

All of these can be valuable achievements.

But they can also become proxies for something else.

Recognition is not necessarily the same as contribution.

An academic can become highly cited while working on questions of limited practical consequence. A startup can attract publicity and raise large amounts of money without creating a sustainable business. A company can command an impressive valuation without having delivered meaningful value to its customers.

The opposite is also true.

Someone might spend years building an obscure but essential technology that improves thousands of lives without ever becoming publicly recognized.

I find that distinction increasingly important.

If I had to choose between building a successful but relatively unknown company that genuinely improves people’s lives and achieving widespread recognition for work of lesser consequence, I would prefer the former.

That doesn’t mean I don’t care about achievement, recognition, or wealth.

I do.

But I want those things to follow from creating something valuable, rather than become substitutes for it.

The goal should be to create exceptional value, not merely to look exceptional.

There’s an additional complication: economic value and societal value do not always align.

A commercially successful product isn’t necessarily beneficial to society. And some of the most important problems, particularly in healthcare and scientific research, may be difficult to monetize.

So maximizing financial upside cannot be the only criterion for choosing what to build.

It has to be balanced against the nature of the value being created.

Why this matters to me as a scientist

My background is in human genetics, computational biology, and increasingly artificial intelligence.

I’ve spent years studying biological problems, developing computational methods, and trying to generate knowledge that could eventually improve human health.

Scientific research is deeply rewarding, but it has also made me appreciate how much work lies between discovering something and translating that discovery into an outcome that matters to people.

A promising result in a paper does not automatically become a diagnostic test.

A machine-learning model that performs well on a benchmark does not automatically improve clinical care.

A scientifically interesting idea does not automatically become something that patients, clinicians, or healthcare organizations can use.

These gaps create difficult problems.

They also create opportunities.

Someone who understands both the underlying science and the practical realities of deploying technology might be able to build something valuable.

But that requires a different kind of work from producing research alone.

It requires understanding what people actually need, who would use the solution, who would pay for it, what existing alternatives are available, and why the product would be adopted.

That’s the kind of challenge I want to explore.

Not because starting a company is inherently more impressive than being a scientist.

But because I am interested in whether my expertise can eventually be translated into something that scales.

And because I want to participate not only in discovering useful knowledge, but also in figuring out how to bring that knowledge into the world.

Ownership without a valuable problem is meaningless

The danger of becoming obsessed with leverage is that it can reverse the proper order of thinking.

You begin with the desire to own a large company.

Then you go searching for something to build.

But a company is not valuable merely because it has a founder, a product, or investors.

It becomes valuable when it solves a problem that matters to someone, in a way that can be sustained economically.

A 50% ownership stake in a company nobody wants is worth very little.

A much smaller stake in an enduring business might be worth an enormous amount.

The relevant question is not simply how much equity can I own?

It’s what valuable thing can I help create, and what ownership structure makes sense for building it?

This has changed how I think about my immediate career choices.

I don’t believe the right next step is necessarily to start a company as quickly as possible.

I would rather spend time identifying a problem that I genuinely believe is important, understanding it unusually well, and testing whether there is a compelling opportunity to solve it.

That might mean speaking with clinicians, studying workflows, building prototypes, testing market demand, or discovering that an idea I initially loved doesn’t work.

I don’t want to spend years defending a hypothesis simply because I was the person who proposed it.

If the evidence changes, I want to change with it.

In a previous essay, I wrote about optimism in the face of uncertainty and the importance of running experiments that generate information.

The same principle applies here.

The point isn’t to make the biggest possible bet immediately.

It’s to find opportunities where experiments are informative, the downside is survivable, and the potential upside justifies increasing your commitment as evidence accumulates.

For now, finding a genuinely worthwhile problem matters more to me than acquiring the title of founder.

Choosing the game

I don’t think there’s a universally optimal career.

A career that feels restrictive to one person might offer exactly the stability, meaning, and professional fulfillment that another person wants.

And entrepreneurship, despite its theoretical upside, can be a terrible choice for someone who values predictability or dislikes the responsibilities of running a business.

Nor do I believe that entrepreneurship is the only way to build wealth.

A high salary, disciplined investing, and decades of compounding can produce substantial financial independence. Many professionals also create enormous impact through their work without owning a company.

But career decisions are not simply exercises in maximizing expected income.

They are decisions about how we want to spend a substantial portion of our lives.

What problems do we want to work on?

How much uncertainty are we willing to tolerate?

How much responsibility do we want to assume?

How important is autonomy?

And what kind of outcome would make the effort worthwhile?

I’ve come to realize that I value ownership, autonomy, and the possibility of disproportionate impact enough that I’m willing to explore a less predictable path.

That doesn’t mean I know I will succeed.

I don’t.

But I would rather investigate the possibility than rule it out simply because a safer, more established career is available.

A high salary can buy comfort, security, and eventually freedom.

Those are enormously valuable things.

But my ambition is to build something whose value can extend beyond my individual contribution, whose potential isn’t predetermined by a compensation ladder, and whose success could give me the freedom and resources to pursue even more important problems.

Ultimately, I don’t just want to optimize for the outcome of my career. I want to choose a game where I have the freedom to experiment, create, take meaningful risks, and pursue something far bigger than myself.

Because the game we choose determines not only what we might achieve, but also who we become along the way.
